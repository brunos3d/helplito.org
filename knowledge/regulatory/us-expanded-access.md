# United States expanded access

Verified 2026-08-24 22:15 BRT (UTC-03:00).

This note summarizes the current FDA process for drugs and biologics. It is not
medical or legal advice and does not assess whether a particular request meets
FDA criteria.

## Scope

FDA describes expanded access, sometimes called compassionate use, as a
potential pathway to an investigational medical product outside a clinical
trial for a serious or immediately life-threatening disease or condition when
no comparable or satisfactory alternative is available.

The available categories include:

- individual-patient expanded access;
- emergency individual-patient expanded access;
- intermediate-size patient population access;
- widespread treatment use.

This record focuses on the first two.

## F-REG-US-001 — A pathway is not product supply

The FDA regulatory pathway exists, but its existence does not mean a
manufacturer has agreed to supply an investigational drug. Likewise, an
expanded access mechanism exists, but that does not mean ION717 is currently
available through expanded access.

```text
FDA regulatory pathway exists
  != manufacturer has agreed to supply the drug

expanded access mechanism exists
  != ION717 is currently available through expanded access
```

## Roles

### Treating licensed physician

FDA says a licensed physician:

- evaluates the option with the patient;
- agrees to oversee treatment and patient care;
- works with the company developing the product;
- submits or coordinates required FDA and IRB paperwork;
- obtains informed consent;
- maintains required records and follow-up reports.

For an individual-patient IND submitted by the physician, the physician becomes
the sponsor-investigator for that use.

### Sponsor or manufacturer

The company must be willing and able to provide the investigational product.
FDA's patient guidance states that expanded access cannot occur without company
cooperation.

For a physician-sponsored individual-patient IND, the company commonly provides
a letter of authorization allowing FDA to reference the company's existing IND.
If no letter is available, enough product information must be submitted for FDA
to assess product quality.

FDA authorization does not make a company supply a product and does not create
an expanded access program.

## Individual-patient IND versus expanded-access protocol

FDA describes two submission structures relevant to individual-patient access:

- A licensed treating physician may submit a new individual-patient IND when
  the access cannot proceed under an existing IND. The physician becomes the
  sponsor-investigator and normally relies on the manufacturer through a letter
  of authorization to the product information in the manufacturer's IND.
- An IND holder may submit an expanded-access protocol to an existing IND. FDA's
  forms guidance distinguishes this protocol route from a new expanded-access
  IND and identifies the forms used by industry sponsors and physicians.

The administrative route does not change the underlying dependency on a
manufacturer that is willing and able to provide the product.

### Institutional Review Board

An IRB reviews the proposed treatment plan and consent under the applicable
process. In a non-emergency request, required IRB review occurs before treatment.
For qualifying emergencies where prior review is not possible, FDA says the
emergency use must be reported to the IRB within five working days.

### FDA

FDA reviews the expanded access request and decides whether it may proceed. In a
non-emergency individual-patient IND, treatment may begin 30 days after FDA
receives the application or earlier if FDA notifies the physician that it may
proceed.

In an emergency, a licensed physician can request authorization by telephone or
another rapid communication method after the company agrees to provide the
product. FDA may authorize use immediately. The written Form FDA 3926 submission
and letter of authorization follow within 15 business days under the process
described on the FDA forms page.

## Non-emergency sequence

At a high level, the current FDA sources describe this dependency chain:

```text
physician and patient consider the option
  -> company agrees to provide the product
  -> product authorization or supporting information is obtained
  -> physician submits the individual-patient request
  -> IRB review and informed consent requirements are met
  -> FDA allows the request to proceed
  -> treatment and required reporting
```

The exact order and submission route can differ when a request is submitted as a
protocol to an existing IND rather than as a new individual-patient IND.

## Emergency difference

Emergency individual-patient use applies when treatment is needed before a
written submission can be made. FDA can authorize by rapid communication, and
treatment may begin immediately after authorization if the product is available.
The physician then completes the written submission and IRB notification within
the stated deadlines.

Emergency processing changes timing. It does not remove the need for company
agreement, FDA authorization, informed consent, oversight or follow-up.

## What the sources do not establish

The general FDA pathway does not establish:

- that a specific manufacturer will provide a product;
- that a specific investigational product has an expanded access program;
- that a physician or IRB will support a request;
- that FDA will allow a particular request to proceed;
- that a product is safe or effective;
- that a particular person is eligible.
